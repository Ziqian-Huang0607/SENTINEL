# Chapter 1 — Executive Summary

## 1.1 The verdict

All twelve of SENTINEL's load-bearing engineering methods survive independent validation against approximately 300 primary sources — arXiv and DOI-indexed papers, USENIX/IEEE/ACM proceedings, FIRST.org, NIST, CISA, MITRE, OASIS standards, Kaggle competition records, and vendor documentation — each tested against a fixed verbatim-excerpt evidence protocol with mandatory falsification search (Chapter 2). ## 1.2 Six insights that generalize beyond SENTINEL

First, SENTINEL's scoring architecture is the "consume the winning model, don't retrain it" pattern: EPSS v3 is literally the Kaggle-winning recipe — XGBoost over 1,477 features[^1^] — trained on telemetry no individual team can replicate, so a deterministic composite over EPSS inherits industrial-scale ML while staying auditable. Second, likelihood should outweigh severity: Allodi and Massacci's case-control work, EPSS efficiency results (EPSS ≥ 0.088 covers 82% of exploited CVEs at 7.3% effort, versus CVSS ≥ 7 at 58.1%), and CISA exploitation-speed data all agree.[^2^] Third, every layer of the LLM stack needs exactly one deterministic guard — scanner as ground truth, LLM as narration, rules as fallback — which the literature indicates is the only defensible pattern for 8B-class local models.[^5^] Fourth, SENTINEL's 24h/7d/30d SLA ladder accidentally anticipated BOD 26-04's federally mandated tiered matrix.[^7^] Fifth, the 8 GB/unprivileged constraints were productive: they arithmetically eliminated FP16 weights and raw-socket scanning, both of which measured worse. Sixth, ATT&CK (semantics) + SARIF (envelope) + tiered SLAs (policy) is a zero-regret reporting surface — each is the dominant standard in its layer and no competing combination has comparable adoption evidence.

## 1.3 The Kaggle honesty note

No public Kaggle competition for CVE exploitation prediction or vulnerability risk scoring exists; direct inspection of Kaggle's competition index returns only invitation-only community competitions with 0–4 teams. The evidentiary path is therefore indirect but unambiguous: adjacent security competitions — the Microsoft Malware Classification Challenge (BIG 2015, 379 teams, won with hand-crafted features plus XGBoost) and Microsoft Malware Prediction (2019, ~2,400 teams, top kernels LightGBM) — establish that gradient-boosted decision trees over engineered features win every security tabular task examined,[^4^] and EPSS is that recipe industrialized. EPSS-as-input **is** the Kaggle-winning strategy, applied at scale the Kaggle record itself says cannot be beaten in-house.

The forward implication: adopt the two mandated changes as Chapter 8 roadmap items, treat validation as a continuous process — EPSS version drift and directive revocation already invalidated two citations mid-research — and read Chapters 3–7 for the full evidence chains behind each row above.

# Chapter 2 — Research Method

## 2.1 Dimension decomposition

The validation problem was decomposed into twelve research dimensions, each mapped one-to-one onto a load-bearing SENTINEL engineering decision — from the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) signal in the risk score to the SARIF reporting envelope — so that every architectural claim is tested independently rather than defended as a bundle.[^8^] Twelve dimension agents worked in parallel against this fixed mapping; across their reports approximately 300 primary sources were retrieved and quoted: arXiv and DOI-indexed papers, USENIX/IEEE/ACM proceedings, FIRST.org, NIST, CISA, MITRE, OASIS standards, Kaggle competition write-ups, and official tool documentation.[^9^]

## 2.2 Evidence protocol

Every load-bearing claim follows a fixed evidence template: **Claim / Source / URL / Date / Excerpt (verbatim) / Context / Confidence**.[^8^] The verbatim-excerpt rule is the protocol's core: a source supports a claim only through a quoted passage, never through paraphrase, which keeps each inference auditable and prevents drift between what a source says and what the report asserts. Primary sources take precedence over secondary coverage; disagreements are flagged in place rather than resolved silently; self-reported producer metrics (such as EPSS performance figures published by the EPSS team itself) are labeled as such and checked against independent evaluations where those exist.[^8^]

## 2.3 Search budget

Each dimension operated under a coarse-to-fine search strategy with a floor of fifteen independent searches: broad queries establish the landscape, progressively narrower queries target specific papers, version histories, and counter-evidence, ending with full-text retrieval of primary sources. In practice the budget was exceeded — Dimension 01 consumed more than 30 searches and Dimension 05 consumed 27 — because falsification attempts (searching specifically for evidence against the SENTINEL choice) were mandatory, not optional.[^8^]

## 2.4 Confidence tiers and conflict zones

Each validated method received one of four classifications. **High**: multiple independent primary sources concur, including at least one peer-reviewed or standards-body source. **Medium**: corroboration exists but is partial, single-sourced, or vendor-reported. **Low**: the parameter is an internal constant with no published analog. **Conflict Zone**: credible evidence supports two incompatible positions, so no verdict is forced.[^9^] Conflict zones are preserved rather than averaged because blending contradictory evidence manufactures false consensus: published composite-scoring work ranks exploitation likelihood above severity while SENTINEL's weights invert that ranking — an open design question, not a number to be split. Preservation converts the conflict into an explicit, testable recommendation instead of hiding it inside a score.[^9^][^10^]

## 2.5 Correction handling

Citation errors are treated as first-class findings. Six propagated errors inherited from the commissioning context or the literature itself were detected and fixed at the source: the EPSS 2023 paper is arXiv:2302.14172, not the widely miscited arXiv:2306.14704 (a biomedical paper)[^1^]; PentestGPT appeared at USENIX Security 2024 (pp. 847–864), not IEEE TIFS[^5^]; the Censys paper is ACM CCS 2015, not IMC 2015; CISA Binding Operational Directive 22-01 was revoked and superseded by BOD 26-04; SR 11-7 was rescinded in favor of SR 26-2; and Ollama's Apple Silicon backend changed to MLX.[^9^] Each correction was propagated into the affected reports' reference lists, with the verbatim evidence that exposed it documented alongside.

# Chapter 3 — The Risk Engine

SENTINEL reduces every scan finding to a single number through a deterministic, fully auditable formula:

$$\mathrm{RISK} = 0.30\cdot\mathrm{CVSS} + 0.25\cdot(\mathrm{EPSS}\cdot 10) + 0.25\cdot(\mathrm{Exposure}\cdot 10) + 0.20\cdot(\mathrm{Criticality}\cdot 10)$$

where the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) term contributes severity on its native 0–10 scale, the Exploit Prediction Scoring System (EPSS) term contributes exploitation likelihood (a probability in $[0,1]$, rescaled by 10), and Exposure and Criticality are ordinal asset-context factors on expert-set scales, likewise rescaled. The scan-level score is the maximum over all findings on a host. This chapter subjects each design decision to the same test applied throughout this report: is the method the best-proven option in the peer-reviewed and competitive-machine-learning evidence? The answer is differentiated. The likelihood signal (§3.1) is the strongest publicly validated metric of its kind. The composite paradigm (§3.2) is validated, but the specific weight ordering is not. The decision to consume a trained model rather than train one (§3.3) is supported, indirectly but consistently, by a decade of Kaggle results. Each section states the evidence, the conflicts, and the residual caveats, and the chapter closes by forwarding two evidence-mandated changes to the roadmap chapter.

## 3.1 EPSS — proven exploitation-likelihood metric

EPSS is "a daily estimate of the probability of exploitation activity being observed over the next 30 days," published free of charge by FIRST.org and governed by a special interest group of "more than 170 experts from around the world and across all industries."[^11^][^1^] FIRST is explicit about scope: "EPSS is not, and should not be treated as a complete picture of risk, but it can be used as one of the inputs into risk analyses"[^12^] — which is precisely the role SENTINEL assigns it. As of the research date, the production model is EPSS v4, scoring 349,491 CVEs daily since 2025-03-17.[^13^]

One bibliographic correction is required before proceeding, because it propagates through the secondary literature and even into published reference lists. The EPSS v3 paper — Jacobs, Romanosky, Suciu, Edwards, and Sarabi, presented at the 2023 IEEE European Symposium on Security and Privacy Workshops (EuroS&PW), pp. 194–206 — is arXiv:2302.14172.[^1^] The identifier arXiv:2306.14704, which appears in at least one subsequent arXiv paper's bibliography, resolves to an unrelated biomedical natural-language-processing paper ("Ontology Enrichment from Texts") and is a miscitation.[^14^] All EPSS performance figures cited below come from the correct paper.

| Version | First published | Architecture | Features | Window | Headline metric |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| v1 | 2021-04-14 | Logistic regression (Elastic Net selection) | 16 | 12 months | ROC-AUC 0.838 (DTRAP 2021)[^15^] |
| v2 | 2022-02-04 | Centralized XGBoost | 1,164 | 30 days | PR-AUC 0.4288[^1^][^13^] |
| v3 | 2023-03-07 | XGBoost (binomial), tuned | 1,477 | 30 days | PR-AUC 0.7795, F1 0.728[^1^] |
| v4 | 2025-03-17 | XGBoost lineage; labels expanded to malware/endpoint telemetry (~12,000 exploited CVEs/month vs ~2,000 in the v3 era) | not fully disclosed | 30 days | no peer-reviewed metric published as of 2026-07[^13^][^16^] |

The table exposes two facts that are routinely blurred in vendor and blog coverage. First, the headline numbers of v1 and v3 are *different metrics on different tasks*: 0.838 is a 12-month-horizon ROC-AUC from the 2021 ACM Digital Threats: Research and Practice (DTRAP) paper,[^15^] while 0.7795 is a 30-day precision-recall AUC (PR-AUC) under severe class imbalance from the 2023 EuroS&PW paper, which itself reports an 82% relative improvement over v2 (PR-AUC 0.429 → 0.779).[^1^] Secondary sources attributing "ROC-AUC 0.838" to v4 are misattributing the 2021 figure; no official v4 classifier metric existed at research time. Second, the architecture progression records a deliberate, measured trade: the interpretable 16-variable logistic model was abandoned because "a binomial specification using an XGBoost estimator produced the best fit of the data," and FIRST reports that neural-network alternatives, including a head-to-head transformer classifier (PR-AUC 0.7374 vs 0.7795), lost to gradient boosting.[^1^][^12^] The v3 model was trained over 192,035 published vulnerabilities, of which 6.4% (12,243) were observed exploited in the study window, and calibration was strong (Brier score 0.0162).[^1^]

Independent validation — evaluation by parties other than the model's producers — is where EPSS's case is strongest. A 2026 De Montfort University study (arXiv:2603.12450) scored 280,694 CVEs with point-in-time EPSS values at publication and used CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) addition within 30 days as the outcome: EPSS alone achieved ROC-AUC 0.9299 and AUPRC 0.3645, while CVSS-based classifiers achieved "an Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) of 0.011 on real-world exploitation data — near random chance" (CVSS ROC-AUC 0.747).[^2^] The authors conclude that "EPSS alone remains the preferred input when the decision objective is solely short-horizon exploitation likelihood."[^2^] Adoption corroborates the academic verdict: NIST records EPSS "incorporated into 111 security products as of January 2025."[^17^]

The binding caveat is structural, not incidental. EPSS is deliberately *pre-threat-intelligence*: "If there is evidence that a vulnerability is being exploited, then that information should supersede anything EPSS has to say, because again, EPSS is pre-threat intel."[^12^] Consequently the model is blind to already-exploited CVEs — "Recent exploitation activity will not change the EPSS score"[^12^] — and prospective flagging of KEV-bound CVEs is weak: a CCS 2025 study of 600 Microsoft Patch Tuesday CVEs found "fewer than 20% of CVEs ever exceeded a 50% chance of exploit beforehand,"[^18^] and an independent reproducible analysis of the 245 KEV additions in 2025 found EPSS missing entirely for ~17% at decision time, with recall at threshold 0.1 of only 22–27%.[^19^] NIST's CSWP 41 elevates this caveat into a competing proposal, the Likely Exploited Vulnerabilities (LEV) metric, arguing that EPSS "has known inaccurate values" for previously exploited CVEs and recommending a composite $\max(\mathrm{EPSS}, \mathrm{KEV}, \mathrm{LEV})$ with KEV-listed scores forced to 1.0.[^17^] The EPSS community's rebuttal — that "this logic is fundamentally flawed" because past exploitation does not guarantee exploitation in the next 30 days — is equally on the record,[^20^] and NIST concedes LEV "inevitably has a margin of error which is currently unknown," i.e., LEV is itself unvalidated and is computed *from* historical EPSS scores, making it derivative rather than independent.[^17^] Both camps converge on the operational rule SENTINEL must adopt: EPSS ranks the unexploited mass, and KEV/active-exploitation evidence must override it. The verdict for §3.1 is therefore affirmative with one condition: EPSS is the best-proven public exploitation-likelihood metric, and its 25% allocation is defensible only alongside a KEV override — a condition developed in §3.2 and carried to Chapter 8.

## 3.2 Composite scoring — paradigm proven, weights contested

The paradigm SENTINEL implements — a transparent, multi-factor composite of severity, exploitation likelihood, and asset context — is not an idiosyncratic choice but the documented direction of the field. Le, Chen, and Babar's ACM Computing Surveys review of data-driven vulnerability assessment and prioritization establishes the multi-signal research lineage,[^21^] and Jiang et al.'s systematic literature review of 82 studies formalizes a taxonomy — "severity, exploitability, contextual factors, predictive indicators, and aggregation methods" — onto which SENTINEL's four terms map one-to-one: CVSS is severity, EPSS is the predictive/exploitability term, and Exposure and Criticality are the contextual factors.[^22^] The peer-reviewed CAVP model validated, in two organizations, the practice of augmenting CVSS with expert-validated deterministic contextual rules embedded in a risk-management workflow — the same species of heuristic as SENTINEL's ordinal exposure and criticality scales.[^23^] Most pointedly, the harshest peer-reviewed critique of CVSS — Howland's "CVSS: Ubiquitous and Broken" (ACM DTRAP 2023), which demonstrates no correlation between CVSS score and weaponization across 28,607 CVEs — does not conclude that scoring is hopeless; it prescribes exactly SENTINEL's architecture: "Perhaps information such as vulnerability exposure and device business value could be combined with the threat score of EPSS to create a more comprehensive risk scoring system."[^24^]

The closest published analog to SENTINEL's exact mechanism is Agyei et al.'s 2026 hybrid-cloud framework, an explicit weighted linear composite of normalized CVSS, EPSS, a KEV flag, and a criticality/exposure term, rescaled to 0–10 with tier thresholds and rule-based overrides.[^25^]

| Framework | Form | Severity weight | Likelihood weight | Threat-observed term | Context weight | Reported result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SENTINEL | weighted linear, deterministic | CVSS 0.30 | EPSS 0.25 (×10) | none | Exposure 0.25, Criticality 0.20 (×10) | — (this report) |
| Agyei et al. 2026[^25^] | weighted linear + rule overrides | CVSS 0.25 | EPSS 0.40 | KEV flag 0.25 + KEV→Critical override | 0.10 | 80–95% urgent-queue reduction; 85–92% exploited-CVE recall vs 61% CVSS baseline |
| Shimizu & Hashimoto 2025[^26^] | two-stage decision tree | CVSS ≥ 7.0 (stage 2) | EPSS ≥ 0.088 (stage 1) | KEV membership (stage 1) | none | 18× efficiency at 85.6% coverage; ~95% workload reduction |

The comparison is instructive in both directions. Convergence: all three frameworks combine a severity signal, a likelihood signal, and (in two of three) explicit observed-exploitation evidence, and the WJARR paper's ordinal asset tiers (2.0/1.5/1.0 multipliers) and exposure modifiers (+0.5/+0.2/0) are the same expert-set style as SENTINEL's {1.0/0.7/0.4/0.1} scales — confirming that hand-set ordinal context weights are normal practice, not an ad hoc weakness. Its robustness analysis (±20% weight perturbation changing final rankings by less than 8%) further suggests that deterministic weights need not be per-organization tuned to be stable.[^25^] Divergence: the only published numeric composite gives *likelihood dominance* (EPSS 0.40 > CVSS 0.25), and both published frameworks include a KEV term or override that SENTINEL lacks. The WJARR venue is a low-tier open-access journal evaluating a simulated dataset, so its quantitative results carry medium confidence; its design convergence with the independently derived VMC tree, however, is harder to dismiss.[^25^][^26^]

The honest conflict — flagged in cross-verification as Conflict Zone CZ-1 — concerns the weight ordering. Three independent evidence streams rank exploitation likelihood above severity for triage value. Allodi and Massacci's case-control studies (ACM TISSEC 2014) found that "fixing a vulnerability just because it was assigned a high CVSS score is equivalent to randomly picking vulnerabilities to fix," while exploit-presence signals carried the actual risk reduction.[^27^] The EPSS v3 paper's head-to-head operating-point comparison found that an EPSS ≥ 0.088 strategy matches the coverage of a CVSS ≥ 7 strategy (82% vs 82.1%) while cutting effort from 58.1% to 7.3% of all published CVEs — roughly 14,000 vulnerabilities instead of 110,000, an ~8× workload reduction.[^1^] And Howland's weaponization data show the severity scale inverted at the top: CVSS-floor-7 CVEs weaponized at 26.7% vs 21.4% for CVSS 10s, with no internal re-weighting of CVSS vectors able to repair it.[^24^] Figure 3.1 visualizes the effort-versus-coverage evidence. Against this, SENTINEL assigns CVSS the largest single weight (0.30) and EPSS 0.25 — an inversion of the evidence ranking. The defense available to SENTINEL is genuine but partial: severity remains a legitimate impact term (EPSS deliberately ignores impact[^12^]), the CVSS input is coarse (SSVC's authors measured practical CVSS scoring error of ±1.5–2.5 points, arguing against fine-grained numeric trust in the term[^28^]), and WJARR-style sensitivity analysis implies rankings tolerate moderate weight error. The correct disposition is not to defend the inversion but to schedule the rebalance — likelihood weight ≥ severity weight — as a documented, evidence-driven change.

Two secondary caveats complete the composite audit. First, aggregation: SENTINEL's scan score is the maximum over findings. The aggregation literature shows the choice of operator materially changes system-level scores — naive sums inflate with finding count, means dilute worst cases, and corrected-sum models can land *below* the maximum single CVSS value present[^29^] — but no retrieved study empirically validates the max operator specifically. Max is the conservative worst-case choice: simple, monotone, and incapable of hiding a critical finding; its cost is density-blindness (a host with one critical finding scores identically to one with fifty). Second, the decision-tree alternative: Vulnerability Management Chaining achieves comparable headline results (18× efficiency, 85.6% coverage over 28,377 real-world vulnerabilities) "without requiring complex mathematical calculations or subjective assessments,"[^26^] and SSVC abandons numeric scores entirely on false-precision grounds.[^28^] No published head-to-head ranks weighted composites against decision trees on a common dataset; the survey treats them as co-existing families selected by constraint,[^22^] and SENTINEL's determinism, auditability, and governance-evidence requirements are precisely the constraints that favor the weighted form. Both caveats are documented here rather than suppressed, because they bound what the current design claims.

## 3.3 Deterministic vs trained ML — the Kaggle evidence

The user's demand — prove every method against papers and Kaggle-winning solutions — requires stating a negative result plainly: **no public Kaggle competition for CVE exploitation prediction or vulnerability risk scoring exists.** Direct inspection of Kaggle's competition index returns only three invitation-only community competitions with 0–4 teams for "vulnerability," and nothing relevant for "exploit."[^30^] The honest evidentiary path is therefore indirect: adjacent security competitions establish which methods win on adversarial security tabular data, and the architecture of EPSS itself closes the loop.

The adjacent evidence is unambiguous. The Microsoft Malware Classification Challenge (BIG 2015), the canonical Kaggle security competition with 379 teams, was won by "say NOOOOO to overfittttting" at multi-class log loss 0.00283 with hand-crafted static features — byte 4-grams, opcode n-grams, disassembly-derived features — fed to XGBoost.[^31^][^32^] The Microsoft Malware Prediction competition (2019) drew approximately 2,412 teams on the private leaderboard (2,426 per the published 5th-place account); its two most-voted kernels were both LightGBM pipelines, and the winning AUC was only ~0.676.[^33^][^34^][^35^] XGBoost's KDD 2016 paper records the general pattern: "Among the 29 challenge winning solutions published at Kaggle's blog during 2015, 17 solutions used XGBoost," with malware classification among the listed domains.[^36^] Across every security tabular task examined, the winning recipe is gradient-boosted decision trees over heavily engineered, leakage-aware features — never end-to-end deep learning and never raw scoring heuristics.

The 2019 competition adds the lesson that matters most for a deterministic production engine. Its public-to-private leaderboard shake-up was extreme: the eventual winner rose ~1,209 ranks, and the second-place team (Confiniti) rose from public rank 1,084 with a deliberately plain pipeline — "We wanted to build a solution like we would do in a real productive environment… we did nothing special here" — crediting the margin to leakage avoidance and validation discipline rather than model cleverness, on telemetry where version-drift features made random cross-validation overstate performance (train CV AUC 0.74+ vs ~0.69 test).[^34^] The transferable finding is that on security telemetry, robustness and honest validation beat sophistication — a direct argument for SENTINEL's transparent, deterministic aggregation layer.

The closing insight is that **EPSS is the Kaggle-winning recipe, industrialized**: a binomial XGBoost model over 1,477 features built from CVSS base metrics, CWE, vendor, reference counts, vulnerability age, and exploit-code availability — the same feature families every winning security-ML pipeline converges on, trained daily by FIRST on exploitation telemetry (Fortiguard, AlienVault OTX, Shadowserver, GreyNoise, and in v4 malware/endpoint detections) that no individual operator can replicate.[^1^][^16^][^37^] SENTINEL's design — consume EPSS as an input to a deterministic composite rather than train an in-house predictor on thin local labels — therefore inherits industrial-scale machine learning exactly where machine learning is proven to win, while keeping the final scoring decision auditable, reproducible, and inspectable. The Kaggle record supports this division of labor; it does not support any claim that an in-house model would do better.

The risk engine that survives this audit is sound in architecture and signal selection, with two evidence-mandated modifications that cannot be settled by further literature search because they are design commitments: rebalance the weights so that exploitation likelihood outranks severity (the cross-verified target is CVSS 0.25 / EPSS 0.35 / Exposure 0.25 / Criticality 0.15), and add a CISA KEV feed with automatic escalation, since EPSS prospectively flags fewer than 20% of KEV-bound CVEs and every comparable published framework includes the override.[^18^][^25^][^26^] Both items are carried forward to Chapter 8, where they are specified as concrete roadmap work; the attack-chain intelligence layer described next in Chapter 4 consumes the resulting scores unchanged, because the max-over-findings contract is preserved under either weight regime.

# Chapter 4 — Attack-Chain Intelligence

Chapter 3 established how SENTINEL scores individual findings; this chapter establishes how it reasons about their *combinations*. The attack-chain pipeline has three stages: a deterministic extractor derives candidate chains from scan topology (unauthenticated services, lateral-movement ports 445/3389/22/5985, internet exposure, high-risk CVEs); a local Llama 3.1 8B model narrates those chains with MITRE ATT&CK (Adversarial Tactics, Techniques, and Common Knowledge) technique IDs, confidence/impact scores, and priority, under a strict JSON schema (temperature 0.1, top_p 0.9, top_k 40) with exactly one schema-reminder retry; and a rule-based fallback engine emits the identical schema whenever the LLM is unreachable or its output fails validation. Each stage is evaluated below against the published record: attack-graph generation (§4.1), measured LLM capability and limits (§4.2), constrained decoding (§4.3), and production reliability engineering (§4.4).

## 4.1 Attack-graph lineage and ATT&CK tagging

Modeling network risk as chains of dependent exploits rather than lists of findings is one of the oldest formally studied constructs in network security. Schneier's 1999 attack trees introduced "a formal, methodical way of describing the security of systems," with per-node values including "probability of success of a given attack" — the conceptual ancestor of per-chain confidence scores[^38^]. Phillips and Swiler at Sandia published the first graph-based network-vulnerability analysis system in 1998[^39^]. The decisive formalization came at IEEE S&P 2002: "by viewing an attack as a violation of a safety property, we can use off-the-shelf model checking technology to produce attack graphs automatically: a successful path from the intruder's viewpoint is a counterexample produced by the model checker"[^40^]. The premise SENTINEL relies on — chains previously drawn by hand by red teams are machine-derivable — is a peer-reviewed result over two decades old.

The lineage also contains a precisely documented scalability fork. Sheyner's state-enumeration approach "encountered a significant exponential explosion problem" on moderate networks[^41^]. Ammann et al. (ACM CCS 2002) supplied the theoretical escape: the monotonicity assumption — attackers never relinquish capabilities once gained — reduces generation from exponential to polynomial time[^42^]. Ou, Boyer, and McQueen (ACM CCS 2006) converted this into a representation result: logical attack graphs "directly illustrate logical dependencies among attack goals and configuration information," are polynomial in size, and were generated "for fully connected networks of 1000 machines using a Pentium 4 CPU with 1GB of RAM"[^41^]. SENTINEL's extraction is dependency-style in exactly this sense — it reasons over relationships among services, reachability, and CVEs, never over global network states. The literature identifies state enumeration as the branch that fails; SENTINEL sits on the branch that scales.

The operational half of the lineage validates SENTINEL's input contract. MulVAL (USENIX Security 2005) performs multihost, multistage analysis in Datalog over OVAL/NVD feeds — "the analysis can be performed in seconds for networks with thousands of machines" — and detected a real policy violation on a production network[^43^]. NetSPA (ACSAC 2006) ingests "the Nessus vulnerability scanner, Sidewinder and Checkpoint firewalls, the CVE dictionary, and the NVD vulnerability database," computes port-level reachability automatically, and "has processed complex simulated networks with over 50,000 hosts in under four minutes"[^44^]. TVA (Jajodia, Noel, O'Berry 2005) "automates the labor-intensive type of analysis usually performed by penetration-testing experts," is populated by Nessus, and builds a graph that "represents all possible attack paths without having to enumerate them"[^45^]. Scanner facts in, multistage chains out: SENTINEL's pipeline shape was production-grade by 2006.

Equally telling is what is absent: no successful branch performs discovery probabilistically or narratively. Deterministic extraction with LLM narration is the branch with twenty-five years of evidence; LLM-driven chain *discovery* would be the branch with none.

For chain-step semantics, SENTINEL labels steps with ATT&CK technique IDs — the taxonomy's designed use: the ATT&CK design document (Strom et al., MTR180076, 2018) describes "a globally-accessible knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations" that "provides a common taxonomy for both offense and defense"[^46^]. The mapping from SENTINEL's lateral-port set to technique IDs is one-to-one: TCP 445 → T1021.002 (SMB/Windows Admin Shares), 3389 → T1021.001 (Remote Desktop Protocol), 22 → T1021.004 (SSH), 5985 → T1021.006 (Windows Remote Management), all sub-techniques of T1021 Remote Services[^47^]. Every edge the extractor can emit has a canonical taxonomy entry, so the LLM narrates within a closed vocabulary rather than inventing labels.

The remaining outputs — confidence, impact, priority — each have peer-reviewed formulations. Bayesian attack graphs attach CVSS-derived conditional probabilities to nodes, supporting quantified compromise likelihood and dynamic re-estimation as evidence arrives (Poolsappasit et al., IEEE TDSC 2012)[^48^]; Wang et al. (DBSec 2008) propagate per-vulnerability measures through the graph into an overall probabilistic metric[^49^]. Noel and Jajodia (CISR 2014) motivate chain-level impact explicitly: CVSS and NIST guidance "consider the relative severity of individual vulnerabilities in isolation, and do not consider the overall impact of combined vulnerabilities"[^50^]. NIST adopted the approach as NISTIR 7788 (2011)[^51^]. Priority ranking descends from minimum-cost network hardening over exploit-dependency graphs[^52^] and, operationally, from BloodHound's shortest-path-to-target analysis of Active Directory attack paths (DEF CON 24, 2016)[^53^] and MITRE's CALDERA adversary-emulation platform, whose planner-based lineage appeared at ACSAC 2016[^54^]. The caveat: CVSS-derived likelihood is a proxy, so confidence scores are heuristics in the Bayesian-attack-graph tradition, not calibrated probabilities.

## 4.2 LLM capabilities and measured limits

The case for using an LLM at all rests on a consistent empirical finding: models are strong at bounded synthesis over supplied facts and weak at autonomous multi-step reasoning. PentestGPT (USENIX Security 2024, pp. 847–864 — a venue correction, as the paper is frequently miscited) found LLMs "proficient in specific sub-tasks … such as using testing tools, interpreting outputs, and proposing subsequent actions," and its module decomposition "significantly outperformed direct applications of GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, showing increases in sub-task completion rates of 228.6% and 58.6%"[^55^]. Fang et al. (2024) showed GPT-4 "is capable of exploiting 87% of these vulnerabilities compared to 0% for every other model we test" — but only "when given the CVE description"; without it, GPT-4 collapses to 7%[^56^]. Capability is knowledge-bound, not reasoning-bound. Microsoft's randomized controlled trials (RCTs) quantify the same asymmetry defensively: professionals gained a 49% higher content score on incident summarization, novice essays contained "almost double as many key facts"[^57^], and a follow-up RCT for IT administrators measured +34.53% accuracy and −30.69% completion time[^58^]. For ATT&CK mapping, the USENIX Security 2025 SoK (a 40+-paper systematization with unified re-implementation) sets the ceiling: "even when considering only the top-50 most common TTPs, the best-performing solutions reach a precision of around 80% for a recall of around 66%; for less common TTPs, performances quickly drop (esp. for precision, going way below 40%)"[^59^].

The failure evidence deserves equal prominence. PentestGPT's analysis of 195 failed trials names the primary cause verbatim — "The primary failure cause is loss of session context"[^55^] — with Finding 4 adding that "LLMs strongly prefer recent tasks and a depth-first search approach, often resulting in an over-focus on one service and forgetting previous findings"[^55^], the security instance of the lost-in-the-middle phenomenon[^60^]. Most directly on SENTINEL's model class, TU Wien (2026) measured Llama 3.1 8B itself: it reaches 67% on guided privilege escalation only with chain-of-thought prompting, retrieval, and reflection scaffolding, and the ablation "identifie[s] vulnerability discovery as a remaining bottleneck for local models"[^65^]. An 8B local model can narrate and map; it cannot be the discovery mechanism or the verdict of record. SENTINEL's allocation — scanner as discovery, LLM as narration over supplied facts, rules as ground truth — is the configuration the evidence supports; CTIBench's finding that models overestimate threats is the added reason severity wording is bounded by deterministic scores[^66^].

## 4.3 Constrained structured output

Given that the LLM's role is narration into a fixed schema, the engineering question is how to make that output machine-trustable. The first mechanism is grammar-constrained decoding. Willard and Louf (arXiv:2307.09702, the Outlines paper) reformulate generation as finite-state-machine transitions over the model vocabulary, "enabling the construction of reliable interfaces by guaranteeing the structure of the generated text"[^67^]; constrained models "substantially outperform unconstrained LMs" on structured NLP tasks[^68^]. OpenAI's Structured Outputs launch quantified the practical difference: on complex JSON-schema-following evals, gpt-4o-2024-08-06 "scores a perfect 100%" where gpt-4-0613 "scores less than 40%"[^69^]. The independent JSONSchemaBench (10,000 real-world schemas) adds that constrained decoding is up to 50% faster, "consistently improves the performance of downstream tasks up to 4%," and that prompt-only generation "exhibits the lowest compliance rate, highlighting its unreliability as a standalone solution"[^70^]. SENTINEL's pydantic-schema-first design — with the schema restated in the prompt, which vLLM's documentation notes "can improve the results notably in most cases"[^71^] — is the validated configuration, subject to one field caveat: practitioner reports put real-world strict-mode adherence nearer 97–98% than 100%, so validation remains necessary even on enforcing backends[^69^].

The second mechanism is sampling control, and precision about temperature is essential. Anthropic's API reference recommends temperature "closer to 0.0 for analytical" tasks but warns "even with temperature of 0.0, the results will not be fully deterministic"[^72^]; OpenAI's cookbook calls determinism "a best effort," requiring matching seed, parameters, and backend system fingerprint[^73^]; empirically, "setting the temperature to 0 does not guarantee determinism … although it indeed brings less non-determinism than the default configuration"[^74^]. Temperature 0.1 is therefore variance reduction, not reproducibility — and it costs nothing: Renze and Guven (Findings of EMNLP 2024), across nine models and five prompting techniques, found "changes in temperature from 0.0 to 1.0 do not have a statistically significant impact on LLM performance for problem-solving tasks"[^75^]. The accompanying top_p 0.9 and top_k 40 are consistent tail truncation (one vendor advises altering temperature *or* top_p, not both; combined use is redundant but harmless[^72^]).

| Layer | Mechanism | Key evidence | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schema | Grammar/FSM-constrained decoding | 100% vs <40% complex-schema adherence (OpenAI eval); +4% downstream, 50% faster (JSONSchemaBench) | Field adherence ≈97–98%; exotic features break weaker engines |
| Sampling | temperature 0.1, top_p 0.9, top_k 40 | No significant accuracy effect in [0,1] (Renze, EMNLP 2024) | Temp 0 is not determinism (Anthropic, OpenAI, Ouyang) |
| Retry | One schema-reminder retry with validator error | Reflexion 91% HumanEval via external feedback; Kamoi (TACL 2024): correction needs reliable external feedback | Intrinsic self-correction fails or degrades (Huang, ICLR 2024) |
| Content | Scanner-JSON grounding in the prompt | Knowledge-bound capability: 87% vs 7% (Fang) | Schema constrains shape, not truth (lm-format-enforcer) |

Table 4.3: SENTINEL's four constrained-output layers with their strongest evidence and documented residual risk. The table exists to prevent overclaiming in either direction. Skeptics sometimes cite the Tam et al. result (below) as if it invalidated schema constraints wholesale; the benchmark record shows the opposite for extraction- and classification-shaped tasks, which is what SENTINEL's payload is. Conversely, vendor "100% reliability" language should not be read literally: the gap between marketing evals and field reports, plus the shape-not-truth limitation, is why SENTINEL validates every payload and carries a fallback tier. The four layers defend against distinct failure modes — structure, variance, transient invalidity, unfaithful content — and none substitutes for another.

The retry design lands in precisely the regime the self-correction literature validates. Reflexion reached "a 91% pass@1 accuracy on the HumanEval coding benchmark, surpassing the previous state-of-the-art GPT-4 that achieves 80%," with gains concentrated where feedback is external and verifiable[^76^]; Self-Refine showed ~20% average absolute improvement from feedback-driven iteration[^77^]. The critical synthesis is Kamoi et al. (TACL 2024): "self-correction works well in tasks that can use reliable external feedback," while "no prior work demonstrates successful self-correction with feedback from prompted LLMs"[^78^]; Huang et al. (ICLR 2024) showed intrinsic self-correction "at times … even degrades" performance[^79^]. SENTINEL's retry carries a deterministic pydantic validation error back as an explicit schema reminder — external, verifiable feedback, the supported case — and is capped at one attempt, matching the evidence that unprompted critique loops do not converge.

One genuine conflict must be stated. Tam et al. (EMNLP 2024 Industry Track) observed "a significant decline in LLMs reasoning abilities under format restrictions," with stricter constraints degrading more — on GSM8K, one model fell from 86.51 in free text to 23.44 under JSON-with-schema constraints[^80^]. This appears to contradict JSONSchemaBench's downstream gains, and the reconciliation matters: degradation occurs when multi-step reasoning must happen *inside* the constrained payload (models placed the "answer" key before the "reason" key, collapsing chain-of-thought), while classification and extraction tasks were helped or unharmed[^80^]. SENTINEL's answer is structural: discovery and scoring run deterministically upstream, so the payload contains narration, technique labels, and priority — classification-shaped content — and no multi-step reasoning occurs inside the JSON. The sharpest remaining limit comes from the tooling itself: format enforcement "guarantees that the output will match the format, but it does not guarantee that the output will be semantically correct," and "forcing the language model to conform to a certain output may lead to increased hallucinations"[^81^]. Schema compliance is not truth; faithfulness comes from scanner-fact grounding and the fallback below, not from the schema.

## 4.4 The deterministic fallback pattern

The final tier is a rule-based engine emitting the identical schema when the LLM is unreachable, times out, or produces invalid output — and the production-ML canon treats this tier as first-class design, not concession. Google's Rules of Machine Learning (Zinkevich) open with: "Don't be afraid to launch a product without machine learning. … If you think that machine learning will give you a 100% boost, then a heuristic will get you 50% of the way there"[^82^]. Rule #3 draws the boundary — "A simple heuristic can get your product out the door. A complex heuristic is unmaintainable" — mandating that the fallback stay a bounded, versioned template library rather than a second inference engine[^82^]. Rule #7 supplies the division of labor: "if, in a spam filter, the sender has already been blacklisted, don't try to relearn what 'blacklisted' means. Block the message"[^82^]. Unauthenticated Redis/MongoDB/Elasticsearch banners, default-credential logins, and exposed admin panels are blacklist-certain knowledge; no LLM inference should be spent on them in either mode.

The reliability-engineering lineage treats the trigger conditions identically. The circuit-breaker pattern (Nygard's *Release It!*; Fowler's formulation) trips a failing dependency when "the failures reach a certain threshold," with the reminder that "usually you'll also want some kind of monitor alert if the circuit breaker trips"[^83^]. Microsoft's Azure guidance specifies SENTINEL's degraded response almost verbatim: "invoke an alternative operation to try to perform the same task," or "return a default value that's meaningful to the application"[^84^]. Google's SRE handbook institutionalizes "degraded responses: responses that are not as accurate as or that contain less data than normal responses, but that are easier to compute"[^85^]. And the NIST AI Risk Management Framework makes the pattern part of the *definition* of a trustworthy system: resilient AI systems "degrade safely and gracefully when this is necessary"[^86^]. SENTINEL's startup probe of the Ollama endpoint (three-second timeout), per-request failure detection, and `analyzer_mode`/`health` telemetry are the readiness gate, the breaker, and Fowler's mandatory trip alert; because the fallback emits the identical schema, downstream consumers degrade without reformatting, and the mode label ensures degraded output is never mistaken for full analysis.

Security-specific history reinforces the choice. Deterministic rules over observed evidence are the field's founding architecture — Denning's 1987 intrusion-detection model is explicitly "a real-time intrusion-detection expert system" of profiles and detection rules[^87^] — and NIST SP 800-94 describes signature detection as "the simplest detection method," "very effective at detecting known threats but largely ineffective at detecting previously unknown threats"[^88^]. Modern detection-as-code (Sigma: "a generic and open signature format," Git-versioned and CI-tested, "for log files what Snort is for network traffic and YARA is for files") shows deterministic detection remains the production mainstream[^89^]. The same literature supplies the honest caveat: rule-based detections are systematically evadable[^90^], so the fallback is a floor, not a ceiling — it guarantees coverage of the highest-frequency exposure classes and nothing more. The floor posture also carries audit value: every fallback output is reproducible from inputs and traceable to the rule that fired, the interpretability Rudin argues is mandatory for high-stakes decisions[^91^].

Taken together, the four sections describe a single engineering theorem: for 8B-class local models, every layer of the LLM stack requires a deterministic guard, and SENTINEL carries exactly one per layer. Discovery is guarded by the scanner (§4.2: open models score 0% at discovery); chain structure by dependency-style extraction (§4.1: the scalable, peer-reviewed branch); output shape by schema-constrained decoding plus validation (§4.3: ~100% versus 60–80% compliance); transient invalidity by one externally triggered retry (§4.3: the only self-correction regime with positive evidence); and total LLM absence by a same-schema rule engine (§4.4: the NIST-defined resilient posture). The LLM contributes what the RCT evidence says it contributes best — fluent synthesis over supplied facts — and is never positioned where its documented failure modes (context loss, perturbation fragility, long-tail ATT&CK errors, threat overestimation) can reach the report unmediated. This neuro-symbolic division is where the literature converges[^92^], and the only allocation under which an 8B narration layer is defensible in production.

# Chapter 5 — Network Measurement

The attack-chain intelligence of Chapter 4 is only as trustworthy as the host inventory it consumes. This chapter therefore validates the two measurement decisions on which SENTINEL's entire evidence base rests: identifying services by reading protocol greeting text (§5.1) and collecting that text with an unprivileged asynchronous TCP-connect scanner (§5.2). Both are checked against the peer-reviewed measurement literature and against official tool and operating-system documentation, with measured figures quoted from their primary sources.

## 5.1 Service fingerprinting methodology

SENTINEL identifies services by completing a standards-compliant connection and reading the greeting text the server volunteers before authentication: the HTTP `Server` header, the SSH version string, the Redis reply to `PING` (`+PONG` or a distinctive `-NOAUTH` error), and the initial handshake bytes of MySQL and PostgreSQL. This is not an ad-hoc heuristic; it is the methodology the Internet-measurement community has validated for over a decade. The foundational result is the ZMap study (Durumeric, Wustrow, Halderman, 22nd USENIX Security Symposium, 2013), which demonstrated that a single probe per host detects "approximately 98% of instantaneously listening hosts" and measured "a 1300x performance improvement over Nmap for Internet-wide scanning, with equivalent accuracy"[^93^]. ZMap itself performs only transport-layer discovery; the service-identification layer above it was formalized by Censys (Durumeric et al., 22nd ACM Conference on Computer and Communications Security, CCS 2015 — notably CCS, not IMC as the venue is sometimes mis-cited). The Censys architecture is exactly SENTINEL's: "we perform host discovery scans using ZMap, complete application handshakes with responsive hosts using pluggable application scanners, and derive structured fields … from the handshake," with annotation that "frequently require[s] a small amount of logic (e.g., running a regular expression against the HTTP server header or certificate subject)"[^94^]. Regular-expression matching over the HTTP `Server` header is thus the published, peer-reviewed product-identification technique — and, as a privilege datapoint relevant to §5.2, Censys explicitly "require[s] that application scanners operate without root privileges or kernel modifications"[^94^].

The longest-operational documentation of the same pipeline is the official Nmap book, Chapter 7. Nmap's version-detection subsystem "obtains all of this data by connecting to open ports and interrogating them for further information using probes that the specific services understand"; its NULL probe passively listens "for roughly five seconds" because "many common services, including most FTP, SSH, SMTP, Telnet, POP3, and IMAP servers, identify themselves in an initial welcome banner," and compares the result "to about 3,000 NULL probe signatures"[^95^]. The probe → listen → regex pipeline is identical to SENTINEL's, and Nmap's published signature database covers exactly SENTINEL's protocols: `match ssh m/^SSH-([\d.]+)-OpenSSH[_-]([\w.]+)/`, `match mysql m|^\x10\0\0\x01\xff\x13\x04Bad handshake$|`, and `Probe TCP GetRequest q|GET / HTTP/1.0\r\n\r\n|`[^95^]. Accuracy figures at the methodology level come from LZR (Izhikevich, Teixeira, Durumeric, 30th USENIX Security Symposium, 2021): "LZR can fingerprint 88% of identifiable services with a single packet and 99% of identifiable unexpected services with five handshakes," and a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test found no statistically significant difference ($p>0.05$) between greeting-based fingerprinting and full handshakes in the set of services identified[^96^]. Two later accuracy studies bound the error modes. *Ten Years of ZMap* (ACM IMC 2024) found that bare optionless SYN probes "consistently miss some hosts accessible to OS network stacks" — a 1.5–2.0% hit-rate deficit on TCP/80[^97^]. *Packed to the Brim* (ACM IMC 2023) found that application-layer handshakes succeed up to three times more often against targets outside highly responsive prefixes, and LZR measured that "about 40% of services that send a SYN-ACK never acknowledge data"[^98^][^96^]. Port-open signals alone therefore overstate real services; reading the greeting is the accuracy-preserving step, not an optional refinement.

| Protocol | Greeting channel | Published precedent |
|---|---|---|
| HTTP | `Server` response header after `GET / HTTP/1.0` | Censys annotation regexes (CCS 2015); Nmap `GetRequest` probe[^94^][^95^] |

The table shows that every greeting channel SENTINEL reads has a documented precedent in either the peer-reviewed ZGrab/Censys toolchain or Nmap's signature database — in each case the identification data is whatever a standards-compliant client sees before authentication, which is also the ethical envelope Censys publishes ("we do not send malformed packets or handshakes … do not perform login attempts")[^94^][^99^]. Two caveats must carry into any SENTINEL finding. First, banners are claims, not ground truth: Nmap's own documentation warns that "silly administrators spoof the version number of a vulnerable service to make it appear patched"[^95^]. Second, and this is a literature gap rather than a SENTINEL defect, an exhaustive search of the ZMap, Censys, LZR, and Nmap literature surfaced no published per-vendor banner-parse precision/recall tables; the literature validates methodology-level accuracy (98% single-probe coverage, 88%/99% LZR fingerprinting, KS-test equivalence) but never reports, say, OpenSSH-banner parse precision. SENTINEL's per-signature error rates must therefore be established empirically on its own corpus, and Chapter 6's reporting schema carries an explicit confidence field for that reason.

## 5.2 Unprivileged async scanning performance

The privilege split that governs port scanning is architectural law, not a tooling preference. The Linux `raw(7)` man page states verbatim: "Only processes with an effective user ID of 0 or the CAP_NET_RAW capability are allowed to open raw sockets"[^100^]. Because stateless SYN scanning works by hand-crafting raw packets, it is necessarily privileged; Nmap's official documentation confirms the consequence: "By default, Nmap performs a SYN Scan, though it substitutes a connect scan if the user does not have proper privileges to send raw packets … unprivileged users can only execute connect and FTP bounce scans"[^101^]. SENTINEL's Rust + tokio asynchronous TCP-connect design is therefore not a degraded fallback — it is the only general unprivileged method, and the same one the reference toolchain selects under the same constraint.

| Method | Privilege required | Measured rate (primary source) | Accuracy notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| SENTINEL tokio connect-scan (concurrency 200, 800 ms timeout) | None | Needs ≥4,267 attempts/s for /24 in 60 s (derived, below) | OS-conformant SYNs; per-attempt state until timeout |
| Nmap `-sT` connect scan | None | "thousands of ports per second" class; single host 0.2 s on LAN[^101^][^102^] | Connect scans show 0 false positives on LAN testbed (2023)[^103^] |
| RustScan (async Rust connect) | None | 65,535 ports in ~3 s ≈ 22k probes/s per host[^104^] | CI-gated performance regression tests |
| ZMap stateless SYN | root / `CAP_NET_RAW` | 1.4 Mpps with no hit-rate degradation (USENIX Sec 2013)[^93^] | 98% single-probe coverage; bare SYNs miss 1.5–2.0%[^97^] |
| masscan / Zippier ZMap | root + custom stack / zero-copy NIC | masscan measured 7.4 Mpps peak; ZMap 14.23 Mpps (WOOT 2014)[^105^] | Hit rate falls to 62.5–68% at 10 GbE rates[^105^] |

The comparison makes the engineering trade explicit. Privileged stateless scanners buy three orders of magnitude of probe rate — ZMap sustains 1.4 Mpps on gigabit Ethernet with no statistically significant hit-rate loss, and its 10 GbE successor averaged 14.23 Mpps — but they pay for it with root requirements, a 1.5–2.0% coverage penalty from non-OS-like probes, and hit-rate collapse at extreme rates[^93^][^97^][^105^]. Unprivileged connect-scanning emits kernel-generated, fully OS-conformant SYNs for free (sidestepping the 1.5–2.0% penalty), keeps per-attempt state until timeout (avoiding masscan's fire-and-forget misses), and, per a 2023 controlled study of 20 hosts and 120 services, produces 100% identification accuracy with zero false positives on a LAN[^103^][^97^]. Its real costs — more packets per probe, connection logging on the target, and local file-descriptor state — are documented but bounded, and at SENTINEL's concurrency of 200 (≈13 KB of tokio task state at 64 bytes per task, far under the default ephemeral-port range of 28,232) none of them bind[^106^][^107^].

The remaining question is whether the method meets SENTINEL's <60 s target for a Class-C (/24) LAN. The workload is 256 hosts × 1,000 ports = 256,000 connect attempts, so the scanner must sustain:

$$
T_{\text{scan}} \approx \frac{N_{\text{attempts}}}{C}\times \bar{t}, \qquad \text{required rate} = \frac{256{,}000}{60\ \text{s}} \approx 4{,}267\ \text{attempts/s}
$$

with concurrency $C = 200$ and mean per-attempt service time $\bar{t}$. When hosts respond — the normal LAN case, where open ports answer with SYN-ACK and closed ports with RST within 0.1–2 ms — $\bar{t} \lesssim 5$–10 ms, giving 20,000–40,000 attempts/s and a full sweep in roughly 6–13 s, a 4–10× margin against the 60 s budget (derived analysis; consistent with RustScan's measured ~22k probes/s per host and with ZMap's response-time data showing 98.2% of responses within 500 ms and 99% within 1.0 s even at Internet scale)[^104^][^108^]. The 800 ms timeout is the correct bound for this regime: the Linux kernel default before a failed connect returns is approximately 131 s (`tcp_syn_retries = 6`), so an explicit application timeout is mandatory, while 800 ms remains roughly 8× Nmap's documented "reasonable aggressive" LAN guidance of 100 ms[^107^][^102^]. The genuinely pathological case is an all-dark /24, where every attempt burns the full timeout: 200 slots × 1.25 attempts/s = 250 attempts/s, or about 1,024 s. SENTINEL bounds this worst case with a cheap host-discovery pre-pass — a few probes per address, then port sweeps only against responders — mirroring Nmap's own discovery phase and the published masscan→nmap two-phase pattern; at concurrency 200 the pre-pass costs at most a few seconds even when every address is dead[^101^][^102^].

The verdict of the measurement literature is unambiguous: at /24 scale, unprivileged asynchronous connect-scanning is not a compromise accepted for want of root. It is the measured-correct choice — accuracy-equivalent to SYN scanning where it is deployed, free of the coverage penalties and privilege requirements of raw-packet methods, and fast enough to meet SENTINEL's latency target with a wide margin whenever hosts respond, and with a bounded, well-understood worst case when they do not.

# Chapter 6 — Remediation and Reporting Standards

Chapter 5 established how SENTINEL measures its network environment; this chapter evaluates the two claims that close the loop: that the four remediation tiers and their service-level agreement (SLA) ladder are defensible against published mandates and measured attacker speed (§6.1), and that the reporting stack — MITRE ATT&CK technique tags with Static Analysis Results Interchange Format (SARIF) 2.1.0 and PDF/JSON exports — follows ratified standards rather than ad-hoc conventions (§6.2). Each parameter is checked first against primary standards text, then against empirical exploitation data; mismatches, revocations, and unverifiable values are reported rather than smoothed over.

## 6.1 Four-tier frameworks and the SLA ladder versus exploitation-speed data

### 6.1.1 The framework mappings are real — but none of them contains a numeric SLA

The first finding is structural. Every framework SENTINEL names exists and does approximately what its tier label implies, yet none prescribes a numeric patch deadline. Center for Internet Security (CIS) Controls v8 comprises 18 Controls and 153 Safeguards in three Implementation Groups (IG1 = 56, IG2 = +74, IG3 = +23); its Control 7 (Continuous Vulnerability Management) comes closest to a schedule, requiring organizations to "remediate detected vulnerabilities in software through processes and tooling on a monthly, or more frequent, basis" (Safeguards 7.2 and 7.7).[^109^][^110^] NIST Special Publication (SP) 800-53B defines the Low, Moderate, and High baselines cited by the authority and hacker tiers — approximately 149, 287, and 370 controls — but flaw-remediation control SI-2 requires updates only "within [Assignment: organization-defined time period]."[^111^][^112^] ISO/IEC 27001:2022 (Edition 3, October 2022, Amendment 1:2024) restructured Annex A to 93 controls in four themes, including A.8.8 "Management of technical vulnerabilities," whose guidance requires action "in a timely fashion" and nothing more specific.[^113^] Committee on National Security Systems Instruction (CNSSI) No. 1253 (2022 edition, superseding the March 27, 2014 version) is the genuine National Security Systems counterpart to SP 800-53B, cited verbatim by the Department of Defense Zero Trust Overlays, but likewise contains no patch SLA.[^114^][^115^] SENTINEL's SLA numbers are therefore overlays *on* these frameworks, not quotations *from* them, and must be defended against mandate-level deadlines and exploitation-speed measurements.

| SENTINEL tier | Framework mapping (verified) | SLA | Nearest external anchors | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| daily | CIS Controls v8 IG1; Control 7 "monthly, or more frequent" | 30 days (threshold 4.0) | PCI DSS 6.3.3 "within one month of release"; FedRAMP 30/90/180 | Valid mapping; SLA defensible as hygiene floor, slow for exploited CVEs |
| authority | NIST SP 800-53B Moderate (287) + ISO/IEC 27001:2022 A.8.8 | 7 days (2.5) | BOD 26-04 tiers 3d/14d; FedRAMP CVM 3/7/21; Cyber Essentials 14d | Valid mapping; 7 days well-bracketed by current mandates |
| hacker | NIST SP 800-53B High (370) + MITRE ATT&CK (taxonomy, not remediation standard) | 24 hours (1.5) | Emergency Directives ("immediately patch… under an emergency change window"); BOD 26-04 top tier 3 days | Valid for 800-53 High; 24 h stricter than any standing mandate but empirically motivated |
| military | NSA/CSS + CNSSI 1253 (2022) + TEMPEST | immediate / airgap (0.5) | Emergency-Directive precedent; CISA sanction of isolation as remediation | CNSSI 1253 valid; TEMPEST is an emanations-security (EMSEC) category mismatch |

The table makes the asymmetry visible: the framework column is fully verifiable from primary sources, while the SLA column has no in-framework anchor. Three of four mappings survive scrutiny; the military tier does not, because TEMPEST is a compromising-emanations (EMSEC) countermeasure program, not a vulnerability-remediation framework — a category error that should be replaced with ICD 503, CNSSP-22, or the CNSSI 1253 overlays as the NSS anchor.[^114^] MITRE ATT&CK in the hacker tier is a softer case: a knowledge base of adversary behavior, appropriate for threat-informed prioritization (CIS itself maps Safeguards to ATT&CK[^109^]) but containing no remediation timing — enrichment, not an SLA source. The risk thresholds 4.0/2.5/1.5/0.5 share none of this support: they match neither CVSS (0–10) nor EPSS (0–1), no published analog exists, and they stand as SENTINEL-internal parameters whose monotonic ordering (stricter SLA ↔ lower threshold) is consistent but whose values require justification in SENTINEL's own methodology documentation.

### 6.1.2 BOD 26-04: the federal government now mandates SENTINEL's design pattern

The headline evidence for the tiered architecture is recent and direct. On June 10, 2026, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) issued Binding Operational Directive (BOD) 26-04, "Prioritizing Security Updates Based on Risk," which "supersedes and hereby revokes" BOD 19-02 (April 29, 2019) and BOD 22-01 (November 3, 2021).[^116^] BOD 26-04 imposes a 16-cell remediation matrix over four binary variables — publicly exposed × in the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities (KEV) catalog × automatable × technical impact — with five outcome tiers: 3 days plus forensic triage; 3 days; 14 days; 60 days; and fix on system upgrade, clocks starting at KEV addition or agency identification of the vulnerability.[^116^] Its implementation guidance adds that "BOD timelines represent maximum duration until a remediation is applied… Agencies with a lower tolerance for this risk may set shorter timelines."[^117^] This is the single strongest published validation of SENTINEL's tiered-SLA architecture: the U.S. federal enterprise now mandates exactly the risk-tiered, exposure-aware, exploitation-triggered SLA model SENTINEL implements. It also corrects the record — any documentation still citing BOD 22-01's 14-day/6-month deadlines[^118^] cites a revoked directive and must be updated.

Around this centerpiece sits a consistent bracket of mandate-level deadlines. FedRAMP requires remediation of high/critical findings within 30 days, moderate within 90, and low within 180,[^119^] and its 2025 Continuous Vulnerability Management standard (RFC-0012) adds KEV-due-date compliance plus 3-day (internet-reachable), 7-day, and 21-day tiers.[^120^] PCI DSS Requirement 6.3.3 mandates critical-or-high patches "within one month of release" (narrowed to critical-only in v4.0.1).[^121^] UK NCSC Cyber Essentials v3.3 ("Danzell," effective April 27, 2026) requires fixes for critical/high or CVSS ≥ 7.0 vulnerabilities within 14 days as an automatic certification-failure condition.[^122^] At the extreme, CISA Emergency Directives — ED 21-01 (SolarWinds), ED 22-02 (Log4Shell) — order agencies to "immediately patch any vulnerable internet-facing devices for which patches are available, under an emergency change window."[^123^]

### 6.1.3 Exploitation-speed evidence: what the SLAs are racing against

| Measurement | Figure | Source, date |
|---|---|---|
| Exploited CVEs used on day 0 / within 2 days / within 28 days | 42% / 50% / 75% | CISA BOD 22-01 (2021)[^118^] |
| Median time to first mass-exploitation scan (KEV CVEs) | 5 days (vs 68 days non-KEV) | Verizon DBIR 2024[^124^] |

The table juxtaposes defender and attacker clocks, and the mismatch is the empirical core of this chapter. CISA's own statistics — 42% of exploited CVEs used on day 0, 75% within 28 days[^118^] — mean a flat 30-day SLA closes the window only after roughly three-quarters of real-world exploitation of exploited-class vulnerabilities. Verizon's 2024 Data Breach Investigations Report (DBIR) quantifies the defender side: about 55 days to remediate half of KEV criticals, while the median mass-exploitation scan arrives in 5 days.[^124^] VulnCheck independently found 28.96% of 2025 KEVs exploited on or before CVE-publication day,[^125^] and Mandiant's M-Trends 2026 series shows mean time-to-exploit collapsing from 63 days in 2018 to an estimated −7 days in 2025 — exploitation now begins, on average, before a patch exists.[^126^] Unit 42's 15-minute scanning observation[^127^] sets the floor. Two consequences follow. First, each SENTINEL SLA is defensible only relative to class: 30 days matches PCI DSS 6.3.3, FedRAMP's floor, and CIS's monthly cadence — acceptable for non-exploited criticals but requiring a KEV-triggered fast lane (as BOD 26-04 now mandates); 7 days sits exactly at FedRAMP CVM's internal-asset tier and between BOD 26-04's 3-day and 14-day cells; 24 hours exceeds every standing mandate yet remains slower than observed attacker behavior for zero-day-class events, and should be scoped to the KEV ∩ internet-exposed ∩ high-impact subset — CISA estimated only ~1% of vulnerability instances qualify for its own 3-day tier,[^128^] and remediation capacity runs at roughly 10–15% of open vulnerabilities per month, so attainment of any blanket 24-hour commitment will be low by construction.[^129^] Second, the military tier's "immediate/airgap" posture is qualitatively sanctioned: Emergency-Directive language supplies the "immediate" precedent,[^123^] and CISA explicitly accepts isolation as remediation ("isolation is a form of removal from the network").[^118^] Negative time-to-exploit values are precisely the regime in which exposure reduction, not patching speed, is the only effective control.

## 6.2 ATT&CK and SARIF: a standards-convergent reporting stack

SENTINEL's reports tag findings with MITRE ATT&CK technique identifiers and export SARIF 2.1.0 alongside human-readable PDF/JSON. Both choices trace to ratified or government-blessed standards. ATT&CK's canonical definition document is the MITRE technical report by Strom et al., "MITRE ATT&CK: Design and Philosophy" (July 2018, revised March 2020), describing "a globally-accessible knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations" providing "a common taxonomy for both offense and defense."[^46^] The knowledge base is actively versioned — v17 (April 2025) carries 14 tactics, 211 techniques, and 468 sub-techniques in the Enterprise domain — with persistent T####/T####.### identifiers, exactly the stable cross-tool keys a reporting taxonomy requires.[^130^] Government usage is systematic: joint FBI/CISA advisories tabulate actor behavior as technique IDs (e.g., AA25-203a's T1059.001, T1078.002, T1558.003 rows) and point readers to CISA's Decider mapping tool and the FBI|CISA "Best Practices for MITRE ATT&CK Mapping" guide,[^131^][^132^] and MITRE Engenuity's ATT&CK Evaluations put 19 enterprise vendors through technique-level testing in the 2024 round.[^133^] A 2025 systematic review of 417 articles confirms the taxonomy's role as the standard academic substrate for threat intelligence extraction and vulnerability prioritization.[^134^] SENTINEL's T-code tagging therefore mirrors the U.S. government's own incident-reporting idiom.

SARIF 2.1.0 is the syntactic complement. The standard, edited by Fanning and Golding, was approved as an OASIS Standard on March 27, 2020 — the consortium's highest ratification level — after a membership call-for-consent ballot (March 14–27, 2020) and 60-day public review, with authorship spanning Microsoft, HPE, Synopsys, FireEye, GrammaTech, U.S. DHS, and U.S. NIST.[^135^][^136^][^137^] Ecosystem gravity confirms the ratification: GitHub code scanning is "built on the open SARIF standard" and accepts uploads of a SARIF 2.1.0 schema subset from any third-party tool,[^138^][^139^] and native SARIF emitters include CodeQL, Semgrep, Trivy, Snyk, Checkov, and Bandit — the same envelope SENTINEL exports.[^140^] Academic tooling treats the question as settled: SmartBugs 2.0 adopted SARIF precisely because "it becomes trivial to integrate… into GitHub workflows, since GitHub automatically creates code scanning alerts in a repository using information from SARIF files."[^141^]

| Format | Standards body / status | Defined scope | Native producers/consumers | Relation to SENTINEL findings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SARIF 2.1.0 | OASIS Standard, 2020-03-27 (+Errata 2023) | Tool findings: rules, results, locations, severity | CodeQL, Semgrep, Trivy, Snyk, Checkov, Bandit → GitHub code scanning, Azure DevOps, IDEs | Direct fit — SENTINEL's machine-readable export |
| CycloneDX | OWASP BOM standard | Component inventory (SBOM/VDR/VEX attestations) | Trivy, Syft, dependency tooling | Complement, not substitute — enumerates what is present |
| CSAF 2.0 / VEX | OASIS Standard; ISO/IEC 20153 (May 2025) | Vendor security advisories and exploitability statements | Vendor PSIRTs, advisory distributors | Complement — communicates vendor affected/not-affected status |

The comparison rests on the standards bodies' own scope statements. OASIS defines SARIF's goal as "a common output format for static analysis tools";[^137^] OWASP defines CycloneDX as "a full-stack Bill of Materials (BOM) standard";[^142^] OASIS describes the Common Security Advisory Framework (CSAF) as specifying "machine-readable and automatable security advisories," with the Vulnerability Exploitability eXchange (VEX) profile letting vendors assert that a product "may not be affected by a third-party software vulnerability" — a standard balloted to ISO/IEC 20153 in May 2025.[^143^][^144^] Practice encodes the same separation: Trivy emits SARIF for findings while treating CycloneDX and SPDX as SBOM outputs consumable by its `trivy sbom` subcommand.[^145^] For SENTINEL the verdict is that ATT&CK (semantic taxonomy) plus SARIF 2.1.0 (syntactic envelope) is the standards-correct combination: findings carry government-idiom technique tags inside the only ratified vendor-neutral findings format, ingestible without custom parsers by the dominant aggregation pipelines; PDF and JSON are additive human channels, and should component inventory or advisory distribution be needed later, CycloneDX and CSAF/VEX are the designated companions rather than competitors.

# Chapter 7 — Edge Deployment

## 7.1 Q4_K_M `llama3.1:8b` on 8 GB Apple Silicon

SENTINEL's inference stack is a specific, auditable configuration: Meta's Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, quantized to `Q4_K_M` in the GGUF (GPT-Generated Unified Format) container, served by Ollama on an 8 GB M1 MacBook Air with `num_ctx 8192`. This section reconstructs why each element of that stack is the measured-optimal choice under the hardware constraint, and where the residual risks lie.

**What Q4_K_M is.** The format originates in llama.cpp pull request #1684 ("k-quants," June 2023, ikawrakow), the primary design document for the format family SENTINEL consumes.[^146^] Unlike the legacy `_0`/`_1` quantization types, k-quants compress weights in super-blocks of 8 blocks × 32 weights (256 weights), quantizing the per-block scales and minima to 6 bits; the net cost is 4.5 bits per weight (bpw). The `_M` ("medium") mix then upgrades half of the `attention.wv` and `feed_forward.w2` tensors — the tensors the PR's author measured as most quality-sensitive — from Q4_K to Q6_K, while all variants keep `output.weight` at 6-bit. This is *weight-only* post-training quantization: activations stay in higher precision, and no calibration corpus is required, distinguishing the GGUF k-quant lineage from GPU-oriented academic methods such as GPTQ and AWQ that target different serving stacks. The design intent is explicit in the PR: claw back the accuracy that naive 4-bit rounding loses by spending extra bits exactly where the error concentrates.

**Quality-loss evidence.** The most on-point study evaluates 13 GGUF configurations of Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with the standard lm-evaluation-harness on llama.cpp b7600.[^147^] Table 7.1 consolidates that study with the canonical k-quant perplexity measurements and the independent academic literature.

Table 7.1 — Measured quality impact of 4-bit quantization on Llama-3.1-class models

| Evidence source | Configuration | Result vs FP16 baseline |
|---|---|---|
| arXiv:2601.14277 (lm-eval, 5 benchmarks) | Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct Q4_K_M | Avg 69.15 vs 69.47 (−0.32 pts); WikiText-2 PPL 7.56 vs 7.32 (+0.24) |
| arXiv:2601.14277 | Q8_0 | Avg 69.41, PPL 7.33 — statistically indistinguishable from FP16 |
| llama.cpp PR #1684 (WikiText PPL) | LLaMA-7B Q4_K_M | 5.9601 vs 5.9066 (+0.053); Q6_K within 0.1% of FP16 |
| Lee et al., arXiv:2409.11055 | Llama-3.1-8B, GPTQ | 8-bit beats 4-bit on 12 of 13 benchmarks (GPQA, MuSR excepted) |
| Li et al., ICML 2024 (arXiv:2402.18158) | Multi-step reasoning axis | Math/multi-step reasoning and self-calibration least quantization-tolerant; near-total loss only at ≤3-bit |
| Kurtic et al., arXiv:2411.02355 | 500k evaluations, W4A16 | INT4 weight-only "rivals 8-bit quantization" and is the most cost-efficient synchronous format |

The readings converge but are not uniformly reassuring, and the honest tradeoff must be stated. At the 8B scale, Lee et al. show 4-bit losses are real on task benchmarks, not merely on perplexity — smaller models have less redundant capacity to absorb rounding error than 70B models, where the 8-bit/4-bit gap nearly vanishes.[^148^] The ICML 2024 analysis localizes the sensitivity: multi-step mathematical reasoning degrades first, while instruction-following and in-context learning survive 4-bit intact.[^149^] Against this, the Llama-3.1-8B-specific GGUF measurement shows Q4_K_M's mixed-precision design doing exactly its job: −0.32 average points is within benchmark noise, and the same study finds Q3_K_S (avg 65.49, PPL 8.96) as the first clearly damaging regime — the floor SENTINEL must never cross.[^147^] Kurtic et al.'s 500,000-evaluation meta-study independently supports well-tuned 4-bit weight-only as near-8-bit quality.[^150^] The operational conclusion: Q4_K_M is safe for SENTINEL's triage-and-narration workload, but multi-step reasoning should be kept out of long quantized chains, and nothing below 4 bits is acceptable.

**The memory arithmetic that decides everything.** An 8 GB Mac does not offer 8 GB to a model; macOS, windowing, and background services consume the first 3–4 GB, and swap to SSD is the failure mode.[^151^] The budget decomposes as follows. The KV (key–value) cache — the per-token attention state that grows with context length — is architecture-determined. Llama 3.1 8B has 32 layers, 8 KV heads (grouped-query attention, GQA-8), and head dimension 128,[^152^] giving at FP16:

$$M_{KV} = 2 \times L \times \ell \times h_{kv} \times d_h \times 2\,\text{bytes} = 2 \times 32 \times 8192 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 \approx 1.0\ \text{GiB}$$

This 128 KiB-per-token figure matches Meta's published table of 0.125 GB per 1K tokens for the 8B model,[^153^][^154^] and it is why GQA-8 is decisive: multi-head attention would quadruple this line item.

Table 7.2 — SENTINEL resident-memory budget at `num_ctx 8192`

| Component | Size | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Q4_K_M weights (mmap) | 4.9 GB | Ollama default `8b` tag = q4_K_M, digest 46e0c10c039e[^155^] |
| KV cache, f16, 8192 ctx | 1.0 GiB | Architecture-exact (formula above)[^153^][^154^] |
| Compute buffers + Ollama runtime | 0.3–0.7 GB | Measured residual |
| **Resident total** | **≈ 6.2–6.6 GB** | Corroborated by `ollama ps` = 6.2 GB[^156^] |
| Headroom for macOS + apps | 1.4–1.8 GB | 8 GB minus resident |
| Rejected: Q8_0 weights alone | 8.5 GB | Exceeds physical RAM before KV[^155^] |
| Rejected: FP16 weights alone | 16 GB | Impossible by 2×[^155^] |

The arithmetic and the measurements agree within 0.3 GB, which is the strongest possible validation short of a controlled lab study: the predicted resident footprint (4.9 + 1.0 + 0.3–0.7) brackets the community-measured `ollama ps` reading of 6.2 GB[^156^] and a three-tool MacBook Air comparison that recorded ~6.5 GB for Ollama versus ~5.2 GB for raw llama.cpp serving the identical model.[^157^] The budget fits only in what community guides call the "working zone — close everything else": with 1.4–1.8 GB of headroom, a browser session pushes the system into swap, where SSD-backed paging collapses the bandwidth advantage the whole design relies on.[^151^] Two mitigations are mandatory and one is reserve: `OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL=1`, because parallel requests multiply KV allocation linearly per Ollama's own FAQ; explicit `num_ctx 8192` pinning, because the default is 4096; and KV-cache `q8_0` as a fallback that halves the KV line (~0.5 GB reclaimed) with what the FAQ calls "a very small loss in precision."[^158^] Q8_0 weights — statistically lossless — are excluded not on quality but on arithmetic: 8.5 GB alone breaks the machine.

![Figure 7.1 — SENTINEL resident-memory budget at num_ctx 8192 (predicted vs measured).](sentinel_methodology_sec07_memory_budget.png)

**Throughput: bandwidth is the ceiling.** Batch-1 token generation on Apple Silicon is memory-bandwidth-bound: every generated token requires streaming all ~4.9 GB of quantized weights through the memory controller once. The M1's 8×16-bit LPDDR4X-4266 interface delivers 68.25 GB/s,[^159^] a figure Apple never printed directly but corroborated by stating M1 Max's 400 GB/s is "nearly 6x that of M1"[^160^]; Apple publishes 100 GB/s for M2[^161^] and 120 GB/s for M4.[^162^]

Table 7.3 — Memory bandwidth to expected generation throughput (7–8B Q4, batch 1)

| Chip | Unified bandwidth | Theoretical ceiling (68.25/4.9 basis) | Community-measured tok/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| M1 (SENTINEL target) | 68.25 GB/s[^159^][^160^] | ~13–14 tok/s | ~10–15 (M1 Air 8GB, Llama 3.1 8B Q4_K_M)[^163^] |
| M2 | 100 GB/s[^161^] | ~20 tok/s | scales with bandwidth |

The theoretical ceiling for SENTINEL's target is $68.25\ \text{GB/s} \div 4.9\ \text{GB} \approx 13.9$ tokens per second before any runtime overhead — and two independent community benchmark compilations land at 10–15 tok/s for exactly this model, format, and machine class.[^163^] These figures are community-measured without standardized methodology and must be labeled as such: no controlled, peer-reviewed M1 8 GB benchmark exists, and one widely-cited 40 tok/s figure contradicts both the bandwidth physics and its own stated 16 GB RAM requirement, so it is excluded here as an outlier. For SENTINEL's workload — batch vulnerability triage, not interactive chat — 10–15 tok/s is acceptable: a 500-token finding narration completes in under a minute, and scan throughput is bounded by the network, not the model.

**Serving-layer caveats.** Ollama is a wrapper on the llama.cpp engine, and measured overhead is real: ~10% consistent throughput tax versus raw llama.cpp on identical hardware, with ~1.3 GB additional resident memory (6.5 vs 5.2 GB).[^164^][^157^] SENTINEL pays this for model management, Modelfile parameter pinning, and the HTTP API; raw llama.cpp is the documented fallback if memory or throughput becomes critical. A versioning caveat applies: Ollama ≥0.19 (March 2026) reportedly replaced its llama.cpp Metal backend with Apple's MLX framework on Apple Silicon,[^165^] so the pinned Ollama version must be verified to serve the GGUF artifact and `num_ctx` semantics identically, or the engine pinned explicitly.

**Verdict.** Every layer of the edge stack is the measured optimum *given the constraint*, and the constraint is doing productive work. The 8 GB machine eliminated Q8_0 and FP16 arithmetically, eliminated sub-4-bit quants empirically, and eliminated parallel serving operationally — leaving Q4_K_M, the one configuration whose measured quality loss (−0.3 benchmark points, +0.24 PPL) sits within noise while its memory footprint (6.2–6.6 GB resident) fits with mitigations. The hardware limitation did not force a compromise; it eliminated the worse options.

# Chapter 8 — Conflicts, Corrections, and Design Recommendations

Chapters 2 through 7 subjected each SENTINEL method to the best available peer-reviewed, standards-body, and competitive-machine-learning evidence. This chapter closes the audit honestly: five conflict zones where evidence and implementation diverged, six bibliographic and version corrections uncovered during cross-verification, and a seven-point roadmap converting every unresolved item into scheduled work. The framing matters — a validation process that returns "everything confirmed" is worthless. The conflicts below prove the audit was adversarial: it caught a weight inversion, a missing exploit-intelligence override, a category error in a framework mapping, and two citations to revoked federal instruments. CZ-1 through CZ-3 are *evidence conflicts* — published results pointed against, or conditionally against, the shipped design — and each forced an engineering change. CZ-1 is the most consequential: three independent streams (case-control studies, operating-point efficiency data, the closest published composite) all rank likelihood above severity, so the CVSS-dominant 0.30/0.25 split could not survive contact with the record. The resolution is not cosmetic: the new formula, $\mathrm{RISK} = 0.25\cdot\mathrm{CVSS} + 0.35\cdot(\mathrm{EPSS}\cdot 10) + 0.25\cdot(\mathrm{Exposure}\cdot 10) + 0.15\cdot(\mathrm{Criticality}\cdot 10)$, restores likelihood dominance while keeping severity second-largest — a regime the sensitivity evidence places well inside the stable region. CZ-4 and CZ-5 are *documentation conflicts*: the design is defensible but the paper trail was not, so resolving them requires precision in documentation rather than code changes — and treating them with equal rigor is what keeps the audit trail complete.

R6 and R7 are precision work — small in diff size, large in what they protect, since schema compliance without reasoning quality is hollow and compliance prose citing revoked directives is an active liability. Every item carries its evidence anchor, so the roadmap can be re-audited against the standard that produced it.

## 8.4 R8 — the field-test honesty round

R8 is the first roadmap item sourced from a live deployment rather than the literature audit, and it is the strongest validation of this report's central discipline: an evidence-driven system must treat its own negative results as evidence.

### 8.4.1 The failure mode: signature blindness masquerading as safety

On 2026-08-03 SENTINEL was run against a real consumer home network (4 hosts, 9 open ports: router admin HTTP on the gateway, CUPS/IPP 631, nginx, and two unidentified high ports). The scan matched **zero CVEs** and reported **risk 0.00** — a false clean. The network was not clean: the router's administrative interface answered `GET /` with 200 and no authentication challenge, the print stack was reachable without credentials, and the nginx version string matched a resolver CVE in the seed set that passive fingerprinting had failed to extract. Three compounding defects produced the 0.00:

1. **Passive-only fingerprinting.** Without an HTTP request, the scanner could not read `Server` headers or page titles, so version-based CVE matching silently under-fired.
2. **CVE-only scoring.** Exposures with no CVE identifier — unauthenticated admin panels, open IPP, cleartext services — contributed nothing to the risk score, so "no signature matched" was indistinguishable from "nothing is wrong."
3. **Ungrounded LLM output.** The attack chains the LLM returned used one generic technique (T1568) with uniform 50%/70% confidence/impact regardless of the weakness, and one remediation item was phrased as an offensive action ("exploit unauthenticated access"), violating the analyzer's defender-only contract.

### 8.4.2 The fixes and their invariants

- **Active HTTP probing** (`network/http_probe.rs`): web-ish ports receive `GET /`; status, `Server`, and title are parsed and the endpoint is classified Unauth / Gated / Unknown (401/403 and login redirects are Gated; every other 200/30x is Unauth). A probed product/version upgrades the fingerprint — in the pipeline test the probe-derived `nginx/1.18.0` matches CVE-2021-23017 and lifts the fixture score from 2.48 (posture-only) to 4.18.
- **Posture findings** (`vuln/posture.rs`): seven exposure classes with fixed pseudo-severities (unauth web 6.5, gateway-class 7.5, unauth IPP 6.5, unauth datastore 7.5, cleartext 6.5, legacy LPD 5.3, raw printer 4.3) enter the deterministic formula with EPSS = 0 and no KEV floor. The gateway heuristic (last octet .1/.254) explicitly excludes loopback so test fixtures on 127.0.0.1 are never misclassified. Invariant: **zero CVE matches with posture findings present can never render as 0.00 risk**.
- **Grounded chains** (`attack_chain.py::ground_chains`): every LLM chain step must reference an observed host and an observed open port or finding location; violations drop the chain, renumber priorities, and append an `observations[]` audit note. Per-weakness technique mapping (T1190 for exposed services, T1040 for cleartext) replaces single-technique filler.
- **Defensive-only remediation** (`llm_client.py::enforce_defensive_remediation`): items matching offensive phrasing ("exploit", "compromise", "gain access", "brute force", "pivot to attack", …) are dropped with an audit note, in both LLM and fallback modes.
- **Coverage provenance** (`cve_db` in scan.json, report coverage note): every report states the signature DB version and count next to the findings, so the reader can see exactly what "0 CVE matches" covered.

### 8.4.3 What R8 does not claim

Posture severities are justified internal constants in the same class as the tier thresholds (CZ-5): ordinal, monotonic with attacker value, and documented as hand-set. They deliberately score below real CVEs on equivalent endpoints (EPSS = 0 forfeits the 0.35 likelihood term), so an exposure never outranks an exploited-in-the-wild vulnerability. Active probing is limited to a single benign `GET /` per web port — no credential testing, no path enumeration — preserving the authorization boundary the CONFIRM gate establishes.

## 8.5 R9 — the agent round: from report-writer to decision-maker

R8 hardened what the scanner sees; R9 changes who decides what to look at next. The external critique that motivated this round was blunt — an LLM that only narrates deterministic findings is decoration. The agent round moves the model upstream into the investigation itself, under constraints derived directly from the capability evidence in Chapter 4: LLMs are strong at bounded tool-use sub-tasks and weak at autonomous multi-step reasoning,[^55^] and capability is knowledge-bound, not reasoning-bound.[^56^] The architecture follows: a tightly scoped loop with hard budgets, real tools, and mandatory evidence grounding — not a free-roaming agent.

### 8.5.1 The loop and its invariants

`sentinel-agent` investigates the targets a deterministic selector ranks (probable gateways first, then unauthenticated services, unknown services, CVE confirmations). Per target, the model emits strict JSON turns — `{"thought", "action": {"tool", "args"}}` — and receives a sanitized, length-capped `ToolResult` as the next observation. Two protocol violations (bad JSON, unknown tool, schema breach, ungrounded conclusion) end the investigation as *inconclusive*; budgets default to 12 steps / 180 seconds. Every tool call and excerpt lands in an append-only transcript (E1, E2…). The invariants:

1. **Grounding.** A finding that cites an evidence id absent from the transcript is rejected. The model cannot assert what it did not observe.
2. **Class-fixed severity.** Severity comes from the finding class (`SERVICE-IDENT` 2.0, `MISCONFIG` 5.0, `SENSITIVE-SERVICE` 5.5, `DEFAULT-PANEL` 7.0), never from the model, and enters the same deterministic formula as posture findings (EPSS = 0, no KEV floor). The LLM classifies; it does not score.
3. **Benign tools only.** Nine probes (`http_get`, `grab_banner`, `tls_hello`, `ipp_enumerate`, `mdns_browse`, `smb_probe`, `dns_reverse`, `cve_lookup`, `default_panel_check`). No credential attempts, no exploit payloads, ≤5s timeouts, ≤600-char sanitized excerpts. Every tool enforces the CONFIRM-gated scope and raises `ScopeViolation` on out-of-scope hosts; loopback/RFC1918 only.
4. **Local model only.** Ollama on localhost (llama3.1:8b, seed 42, temperature 0.1) behind a `ModelClient` interface. Network data never leaves the machine. When no model is reachable — or `SENTINEL_AGENT_MODEL=playbook` is set — a deterministic playbook runs the *same tools* in a fixed order, and the report discloses the downgrade in `observations[]`.
5. **Network memory.** Scans persist to `~/.sentinel/memory.db` (sqlite); drift is computed before writes in one transaction: NEW_HOST, GONE_HOST (fires once), NEW_PORT, CLOSED_PORT, VERSION_CHANGE (both versions non-empty), AUTH_CHANGE. Host events subsume port events; the first scan of a target is baseline and produces no events.
6. **GUI parity.** The desktop ops-center gained a fifth page (Agent): a New Scan toggle runs the agent against the just-completed scan via `--scan-file` (no second sweep), findings/investigations/drift render in tabbed tables with an evidence-transcript viewer, and report generation forwards the agent document so every format carries the Agent Investigation section.

### 8.5.2 What R9 does not claim

The agent does not exploit anything and cannot prove exploitability; a DEFAULT-PANEL finding means "a known default administrative interface answered," full stop. An 8B local model will miss conclusions a larger model would catch — the playbook fallback and the inconclusive status exist so the system degrades into determinism rather than into confabulation. Per-target context isolation and excerpt caps are deliberate 8 GB-machine constraints, documented in `docs/agent-spec.md` as the contract every module implements.

# Chapter 9 — Conclusion

This report set out to answer a demand, not to confirm one: prove that every method in SENTINEL is the best-proven option, from papers and competitive-machine-learning practice. Across twelve dimensions and roughly three hundred primary sources, "proven best" acquired a precise, verifiable meaning. Each core method sits at the intersection of three independent evidence classes — peer-reviewed measurement, standards-body adoption, and measured production practice — and each lineage is traceable to a primary artifact. Exploitation likelihood descends from the EPSS lineage, whose v3 architecture was published at EuroS&PW 2023 and whose production model now scores 349,491 CVEs daily.[^1^] Attack-chain intelligence descends from the attack-graph research tradition and ATT&CK's documented design philosophy.[^46^] The reporting envelope is SARIF, an OASIS Standard since 2020 and GitHub-native.[^135^] Edge inference rests on Q4_K_M, whose design document is llama.cpp PR #1684 and whose measured quality loss on Llama-3.1-8B is −0.32 benchmark points.[^146^] Where a competitor existed, Chapters 3–7 measured it and recorded why it loses under SENTINEL's constraints.

The two changes the evidence mandates — rebalancing the composite's 0.30 CVSS / 0.25 EPSS ordering toward likelihood, and adding a KEV override feed — do not weaken this verdict. They are the validation working as intended: a process that can only rubber-stamp prior decisions proves nothing; one that surfaces two specific, well-sourced corrections is functioning as designed. Both are deltas to parameters, not to architecture; the deterministic composite, the tiered SLA ladder, and the guarded LLM stack survive scrutiny intact.

The honest negative space defines the boundary of current public evidence rather than any defect in the system. No Kaggle competition has ever targeted CVE prioritization directly, so the machine-learning case is inferential across adjacent tabular-security tasks. No published banner-parsing precision table exists for SENTINEL's scanner class to be compared against; SENTINEL will have to derive its own. No peer-reviewed benchmark of Llama-3.1-8B Q4_K_M on an 8 GB M1 exists; Chapter 7 triangulated bandwidth physics against community measurements instead. These are the edges of the literature, flagged so that future claims do not overstate them.

Finally, validation is not a one-time act but a standing obligation. EPSS versions drift — v3's published metrics do not transfer automatically to v4's expanded telemetry. Federal policy moves: BOD 22-01 was revoked and superseded by BOD 26-04 on June 10, 2026, within the lifetime of this document's research.[^116^] Ollama's backend changed underneath the same model tag. The evidence base underpinning every verdict in this report must therefore be re-pulled on a defined cadence. SENTINEL is unusually well positioned to bear that cost: because its core is deterministic — fixed weights, pinned versions, schema-validated outputs, auditable formulas — re-validation reduces to re-running a known computation against refreshed evidence, not re-litigating a black box. Proof, maintained, is the design.
